# Robust Speech Recognition and Translation via Large-Scale Weak Supervision

## Abstract

Speech recognition systems that reach human-level accuracy on a single benchmark often degrade sharply when moved to new datasets, recording conditions, or accents, because they are tuned to the fine-grained statistics of one training distribution. This paper presents Whisper, a single encoder-decoder Transformer trained to predict transcripts, translations, timestamps, and spoken-language identity as text, using 680,000 hours of multilingual, multitask audio paired with internet-gathered text, filtered with lightweight, largely automated heuristics rather than manual curation {C001}. Unlike systems built around self-supervised audio pre-training, which still require a fine-tuned decoder for each downstream task, Whisper is evaluated zero-shot: no parameter is updated for any target benchmark. Across 12 diverse speech-recognition datasets, zero-shot Whisper reduces word error rate (WER, the fraction of words transcribed incorrectly) by 55.2% on average relative to a supervised LibriSpeech baseline, despite matching that baseline's roughly mid-2019 state-of-the-art performance on LibriSpeech itself (2.7% WER) {C002}{C003}. On multilingual recognition, error scales with training data per language (r² = 0.83 between log word error rate and log hours) {C004}; on X→English translation, Whisper sets a new zero-shot state of the art of 29.1 BLEU (a standard machine-translation quality score) on the CoVoST2 benchmark {C005}, although the analogous data-scaling correlation is far weaker (r² = 0.24) {C018}. Whisper is also more robust to additive noise than 14 LibriSpeech-trained models {C006}, and on long-form transcription it beats the best open-source system tested and approaches a professional human-transcription baseline {C011}{C012}. These gains scale predictably with training-data quantity and model size {C007}{C008}{C009}. The training data remains English-heavy (65%) with a long tail of low-resource languages, and Whisper still underperforms specialized supervised systems on some benchmarks {C014}{C016}{C020}. The results suggest that scaling weakly supervised pre-training — long used in natural language processing — has been underappreciated as a route to robust, general-purpose speech systems {C015}.

## 1. Introduction

Modern automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems routinely report word error rates (WER) — the fraction of words a transcript gets wrong relative to a reference transcript — that rival or beat individual human transcribers on standard benchmarks such as LibriSpeech. Yet a system that is near-perfect on the exact test set it was tuned for frequently performs far worse when the microphone, accent, background noise, or recording style changes even slightly {C006}. This gap between narrow benchmark competence and broad real-world reliability is the central problem this paper addresses: an ASR model is only as useful as it is *robust*, and robustness has historically required either dataset-specific fine-tuning for every new setting or careful, expensive curation of matched training data.

Two existing strategies partially address this. The first, and more heavily investigated in recent years, is self-supervised (unsupervised) pre-training on unlabeled audio, scaled to as much as 1,000,000 hours of raw speech. This learns strong general-purpose audio representations, but produces only an encoder: the model cannot produce text until a decoder (or separate language model) is fine-tuned on a labeled dataset for each task, which re-introduces the narrow, dataset-specific brittleness the encoder was meant to avoid. The second strategy pools multiple existing *human-labeled* datasets — for example, a roughly 5,140-hour combination across seven datasets — to expose a model to more acoustic and stylistic diversity than any single corpus provides. Prior work of this kind (Narayanan et al., Likhomanenko et al.) shows combining datasets helps generality, but the resulting sets remain small relative to what unsupervised audio pre-training already consumes, and still depend on the availability and cost of gold-standard transcription {C014}.

Neither strategy scales the thing that most directly targets robustness: *supervision itself*, at the scale unsupervised pre-training has already shown is attainable. This is the gap this paper targets. The research question is direct: if audio and text supervision — however noisy, and gathered automatically from the internet rather than transcribed by hand — is scaled by roughly an order of magnitude beyond prior supervised speech work, does the resulting model generalize zero-shot across datasets, languages, and tasks, without any fine-tuning at evaluation time? {C001}

This paper answers that question with Whisper, a single sequence-to-sequence Transformer trained on 680,000 hours of multilingual and multitask audio-text data — 438,218 hours of English speech recognition, 117,113 hours of non-English speech recognition spanning 96 languages, and 125,739 hours of translation data into English — collected from the internet with lightweight, mostly automated filtering rather than manual curation {C001}{C002}. Every task (transcription, translation into English, spoken-language identification, and timestamp prediction) is expressed as one shared text-output format, decoded by one model, and the model is evaluated strictly zero-shot on external benchmarks it was never fine-tuned on.

The central finding is that this scale of weak supervision produces large, consistent robustness gains that a same-benchmark-trained system does not show, and that these gains follow a predictable relationship with both the amount of training data and the size of the model. The paper reports: (i) a 55.2% relative error reduction across 12 diverse recognition datasets despite comparable LibriSpeech performance to a strong supervised baseline {C003}; (ii) a new zero-shot state of the art of 29.1 BLEU on X→English translation {C005}; (iii) noise robustness that exceeds 14 LibriSpeech-trained baselines {C006}; (iv) systematic scaling behavior with training-data quantity and model size across recognition, translation, and language-identification tasks {C004}{C007}{C008}{C009}; and (v) long-form transcription performance that beats the strongest open-source system tested and approaches a professional human-transcription reference {C011}{C012}. The paper also reports where this approach falls short: uneven language coverage from an English-heavy internet corpus, a substantially weaker data-scaling relationship for translation than for recognition, and specific benchmarks (VoxPopuli, Fleurs language identification) on which more specialized supervised systems remain ahead {C014}{C016}{C018}{C020}.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 situates Whisper against unsupervised pre-training and multi-dataset supervised approaches. Section 3 describes the data and model. Section 4 describes the zero-shot evaluation protocol and the controlled scaling studies. Section 5 reports results on recognition robustness, translation, noise, scaling, and long-form transcription. Section 6 discusses what the results imply and where they are limited. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Related Work

**Self-supervised audio pre-training.** The dominant recent strategy for building general-purpose speech models has been to pre-train an encoder on large amounts of unlabeled audio (wav2vec 2.0-style approaches), which has been scaled to as much as 1,000,000 hours of unlabeled speech {E030}. This produces strong acoustic representations, but the encoder alone cannot transcribe: a decoder, or an external language model, must still be fine-tuned on a labeled dataset for every downstream task and language, which re-imposes the narrow, in-distribution brittleness this paper is concerned with. Whisper differs by training an encoder *and* decoder jointly, end-to-end, on supervised (if weakly and automatically labeled) data, so that no fine-tuning step is needed before evaluation.

**Combining existing supervised datasets.** A second line of work increases robustness by pooling several existing human-labeled speech datasets into a larger, more diverse training set — for instance, a roughly 5,140-hour combination across seven datasets. Prior work in this vein (Narayanan et al., Likhomanenko et al.) shows this diversity improves generality relative to training on any one dataset alone. Whisper shares this motivation — diversity beats narrow fit — but is over two orders of magnitude larger, substituting automated web-scale collection with lightweight filtering for the manual transcription that limits how large a purely human-labeled combination can grow {C014}.

**Weak supervision at scale.** The strategy of training directly on noisy, automatically or loosely labeled data rather than gold-standard annotations is well established in natural language processing, but this paper's evidence indicates it has been comparatively underexplored as the primary scaling axis for speech recognition {C015}. Whisper's contribution, in this context, is not a new architecture or training objective — it uses a standard encoder-decoder Transformer {C001}{E003} — but an empirical demonstration of what happens when weak supervision for speech is scaled to a size comparable to what unsupervised audio pre-training has already reached.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Data

Whisper is trained on 680,000 hours of audio paired with text collected from the internet, split into three components: 438,218 hours of English speech paired with English transcripts, 117,113 hours of non-English speech paired with transcripts in the original language (covering 96 languages besides English), and 125,739 hours of audio paired with English translations, predominantly non-English speech translated into English text {C001}{C002}. Rather than relying on costly manual transcription or verification, the data pipeline uses lightweight, largely automated heuristics to filter out likely machine-generated or otherwise low-quality transcript pairs, trading strict curation for scale. Audio is broken into 30-second segments, each paired with the subset of the transcript that falls inside that time window {E026}, which gives the model a fixed-length training unit while still letting it learn to align variable amounts of speech and text.

This is also where the paper's main acknowledged weakness originates: because the source material is drawn from the internet, its language composition mirrors the internet's own imbalance. English data (438,218 hours) dwarfs the non-English total (117,113 hours across 96 languages), so roughly 65% of the dataset by hours is English, and most non-English languages individually have fewer than 1,000 hours {C014}. The automated language identification used to assign non-English audio a language label is itself imperfect: a large share of data nominally labeled Welsh (around 9,000 hours) was actually misclassified English — illustrative of the label noise this collection method introduces {E021}.

### 3.2 Task format and architecture

Whisper casts every task it performs — transcription, translation into English, spoken-language identification, and timestamp prediction — as one shared sequence of output text, predicted by a single decoder conditioned on the audio encoder's output and on special tokens that specify which task and language are being requested. This means one model, decoded once, can be asked to transcribe in the source language, translate into English, or identify the spoken language, without switching architectures or heads.

The model itself is a standard encoder-decoder Transformer, trained at five sizes summarized in Table 1 {C001}{E003}.

**Table 1. Whisper model family.**

| Model | Layers | Width | Attention heads | Parameters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tiny | 4 | 384 | 6 | 39M |
| Base | 6 | 512 | 8 | 74M |
| Small | 12 | 768 | 12 | 244M |
| Medium | 24 | 1024 | 16 | 769M |
| Large | 32 | 1280 | 20 | 1550M |

### 3.3 Training procedure

Models are trained for 220 updates, corresponding to roughly two to three passes over the full 680,000-hour dataset, using the AdamW optimizer with linear learning-rate decay and a batch size of 256 audio segments {E025}. No data augmentation or explicit regularization (such as dropout) is used; instead, generalization is expected to come from the sheer diversity already present in a dataset this large {E029}. This is a deliberate bet distinguishing Whisper from most supervised ASR recipes, which typically rely on augmentation and regularization precisely because their training sets are far smaller.

## 4. Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Zero-shot evaluation protocol

The evaluation strategy that most directly targets this paper's research question is zero-shot transfer: Whisper is evaluated on external benchmarks — LibriSpeech, Common Voice, Fleurs, CoVoST2, VoxPopuli, Multilingual LibriSpeech, TED-LIUM 3 (TED-talk transcripts), CHiME-6, and seven long-form datasets (Kincaid46, Rev16, Earnings calls, CORAAL) — without fine-tuning on their training splits. This is a stricter test than the way most prior ASR systems are evaluated (fine-tuned or otherwise adapted to each benchmark's training distribution), and it is the evaluation regime under which all of this paper's headline comparisons are made {C002}{C003}.

Because word error rate is sensitive to superficial formatting differences (punctuation, capitalization, number formatting) that do not reflect transcription errors, WER is computed after a text-normalization step developed alongside Whisper. This normalizer was jointly developed with the model being evaluated, which creates a risk that it is inadvertently tuned to fix patterns specific to Whisper's own output rather than to normalize fairly across systems {C013, L001}. To check for this, results were cross-checked against an independently developed normalizer (FairSpeech); the two were found broadly similar, though with some differences on specific datasets {E019}. Text normalization can change measured WER substantially — by up to 50% on some datasets — so this choice materially affects the numbers reported below, and it is flagged here as a methodological caveat rather than resolved outright {C013}{L005}.

### 4.2 Controlled scaling studies

Two additional controlled experiments isolate the effect of scale itself. First, a dataset-size scaling study trains medium-sized models with early stopping at six dataset sizes — 0.5%, 1%, 2%, 4%, 8%, and 100% of the full corpus, i.e. 3,405; 6,811; 13,621; 27,243; 54,486; and 681,070 hours — and measures downstream performance at each {E014}. Second, a model-size comparison evaluates all five model sizes (Table 1) on the same task suite {E013}. Together these isolate whether gains come from more data, a bigger model, or both.

### 4.3 Robustness and long-form evaluation

Robustness to acoustic distortion is tested with white noise and "pub" (background babble) noise from the Audio Degradation Toolbox across a range of signal-to-noise ratios (SNR), comparing Whisper's WER against 14 LibriSpeech-trained models {E010}. Long-form transcription — audio substantially longer than the 30-second training segments — is evaluated on seven datasets against the best open-source system tested (an NVIDIA speech-to-text model, Conformer-CTC Large) and several commercial services {E011}. A 25-recording subset of Kincaid46 has professional human transcriptions available, enabling comparison with a computer-assisted and a pure-human transcription service {E012}.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 Zero-shot recognition matches in-distribution performance but not out-of-distribution robustness

On LibriSpeech test-clean — the single most heavily used ASR benchmark, and one that many prior systems are specifically tuned toward — the largest Whisper model (Large V2) achieves 2.7% WER, comparable to systems that represented roughly the state of the art in mid-2019 {C002}. Taken alone, this number would suggest Whisper is a competent but not exceptional recognizer by current benchmark standards.

That framing, however, misses the paper's central point. Averaged across 12 other, more diverse evaluation datasets (including Common Voice, CHiME-6, and TED-LIUM 3) in the same zero-shot setting, Whisper achieves a 12.8% average WER against a 29.3% average WER for a wav2vec 2.0 Large model with no language model, evaluated under the same conditions — a 55.2% relative error reduction {C003}. In other words, a supervised system tuned to do well specifically on LibriSpeech reaches a lower WER on LibriSpeech itself, but loses substantially more accuracy than Whisper does when the evaluation distribution shifts even slightly away from that one dataset. This pattern is consistent with the paper's motivating claim: matched-benchmark competence and cross-distribution robustness are different properties, and training on a very large and diverse (if noisier) supervision set favors the latter {C003}{C006}.

Whisper's robustness advantage extends to controlled acoustic degradation. Under additive pub noise, all 14 compared LibriSpeech-trained models degrade faster than Whisper as noise intensity increases, and below an SNR of 10 dB every one of them performs worse than Whisper {C006}. This strengthens the same interpretation: robustness to conditions unlike the training distribution, rather than peak accuracy on a matched benchmark, is where the scale-of-supervision strategy shows its largest advantage.

### 5.2 Multilingual recognition scales predictably with data; translation scales more weakly

Across 75 languages on the Fleurs multilingual benchmark, WER and the amount of Whisper training data available for that language are strongly related: the squared correlation coefficient (r²) between log(WER) and log(training hours per language) is 0.83, and WER roughly halves for every 16-fold increase in per-language training data {C004}. This is a clean, monotonic scaling relationship, and it directly explains much of the paper's central limitation: because per-language data is so uneven (Section 3.1), so is per-language accuracy.

The equivalent relationship for X→English translation is far weaker: the squared correlation coefficient between log(translation training hours) and zero-shot BLEU is only 0.24, less than a third of the recognition figure {C018}. The evidence attributes this gap not to translation being intrinsically harder to scale, but to noisier training labels for translation data — in particular, errors in the automated audio language-identification step used to build the translation training set, which is the same class of error responsible for the Welsh-mislabeled-as-English case noted in Section 3.1 {C018}{L007}. Despite this weaker scaling relationship, Whisper still achieves a new zero-shot state of the art of 29.1 BLEU on the CoVoST2 X→English translation benchmark, using none of CoVoST2's own training data — a result built on 68,000 hours of X→English translation data in pre-training against CoVoST2's own 861 hours {C005}. The authors caution that this state-of-the-art claim uses the same custom text normalizer discussed in Section 4.1, which prevents a fully like-for-like comparison against prior work's reported numbers {C005}{L002}.

### 5.3 Where Whisper falls short

Not every comparison favors Whisper, and the evidence explicitly reports the negative results. On VoxPopuli, Whisper's zero-shot WER of 13.6% is substantially worse than XLS-R (10.6%) and mSLAM (9.1%), beating only a weaker fine-tuned baseline (VP-10K+FT) {C016}. The authors suspect this may partly reflect other systems having included VoxPopuli's own distribution in their unsupervised pre-training, making the comparison less like-for-like than it appears {C016}{L008}. On Fleurs language identification, Whisper reaches 64.5% accuracy against a 77.7% supervised state of the art on the full 102-language set; restricted to the 82 languages present in Whisper's training data, accuracy rises to 80.3%, still below that state of the art and structurally capped by 20 of the 102 Fleurs languages having no Whisper training data at all {C020}. On Multilingual LibriSpeech, Whisper (7.3% WER) outperforms XLS-R (9.7%) but only matches the Maestro system (also 7.3%) {E032, reported result}. Together these show: where a specialized system has matched or targeted supervision for a specific benchmark, it can still match or beat Whisper's broad-coverage approach.

### 5.4 Scaling with data and model size

The controlled dataset-size sweep (Section 4.2) shows a consistent shape across three tasks (Table 2): rapid improvement from the smallest training sizes tested up through roughly 13,000-54,000 hours, followed by markedly diminishing returns as the dataset grows to its full 681,070-hour size {C008}{C009}{C019}.

**Table 2. Performance vs. training-data size (medium-sized models, early-stopped).**

| Dataset size (hours) | 3,405 | 6,811 | 13,621 | 27,243 | 54,486 | 681,070 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| English WER (↓) | 30.5 | 19.6 | 14.4 | 12.3 | 10.9 | 9.9 |
| Multilingual WER (↓) | 92.4 | 72.7 | 56.6 | 45.0 | 36.4 | 29.2 |
| X→En BLEU (↑) | 0.2 | 1.7 | 7.9 | 13.9 | 19.2 | 24.8 |

The English-recognition curve moves from 30.5% WER at 3,405 hours to 9.9% at the full 681,070 hours, with the steepest gains occurring between roughly 3,000 and 13,000 hours and comparatively small further gains from 54,486 to 681,070 hours {C008}. Multilingual recognition and translation follow the same qualitative shape: multilingual WER falls from 92.4% to 29.2%, and X→English BLEU rises from 0.2 to 24.8 across the same six sizes {C009}{C019}. The evidence available does not fully disentangle whether the flattening near full data size reflects an intrinsic saturation of what this kind of weak supervision can achieve, data quality limits, or under-training at that scale; the authors note that further analysis is needed to characterize scaling behavior for speech recognition more precisely {L010}.

Increasing model size (Table 1) produces a similar monotonic pattern: English WER on LibriSpeech test-clean falls from 6.7% for the smallest (Tiny) model to 4.2% for the largest (Large V2), and comparable improvements with size are observed across multilingual recognition, translation, and language identification {C007}. A related but distinct scaling effect concerns *how* training is structured rather than how much data or how large a model is used: training one model jointly on multiple tasks and languages, versus training a comparably sized English-only model on the same compute budget, shows negative transfer (the joint model underperforms) at small model and compute scales, but the relationship crosses over so that the joint model outperforms its English-only counterpart at the largest scales tested {C010}. This crossover is a specific and practically relevant finding for anyone deciding whether to train separate single-task models or one multitask model: the answer, per this evidence, depends on the compute budget available.

### 5.5 Long-form transcription

Long-form audio — substantially longer than the 30-second segments used in training — requires the model to be run repeatedly and its outputs stitched together, which introduces failure modes (looping, drift) not present in short-segment evaluation. An ablation over the long-form decoding pipeline shows each added heuristic reduces average WER across the seven datasets: 11.0% with greedy decoding, 10.6% adding beam search, 10.6% adding temperature-fallback scheduling, 10.2% adding voice-activity-detection segment boundaries, 10.0% adding previous-segment-text conditioning, and 10.0% adding an initial-timestamp constraint {C017}. The authors describe these heuristics as workarounds for noisy model predictions in this longer-context setting rather than as evidence the underlying model itself handles long audio natively {L009}.

With this full pipeline, Whisper outperforms the strongest open-source system tested (an NVIDIA STT Conformer-CTC Large model) on all seven long-form datasets {C011}. Some commercial ASR services in this comparison may themselves have been trained on portions of these same public datasets, limiting how cleanly the comparison isolates zero-shot generalization {C011}{L003}. On a 25-recording, human-transcribed subset of Kincaid46, Whisper's WER is close to but not better than a professional, computer-assisted transcription service: that service's aggregate WER is 1.15 percentage points lower than Whisper's {C012}. With only 25 recordings from a single dataset, this should be read as a narrow data point on closeness to human-level performance, not a general claim about human parity across domains {C012}{L004}.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 What the results suggest

Taken together, the results in Section 5 point to a consistent interpretation: robustness in speech recognition — the ability to keep working when the input distribution shifts away from any single training or evaluation set — can be obtained primarily by scaling the *breadth and diversity* of weakly supervised training data, rather than by careful curation and fine-tuning on each new target distribution {C003}{C006}{C015}. This is visible most directly in the contrast between Whisper's merely competitive LibriSpeech-only performance and its large advantage on the 12-dataset average (Section 5.1), and it is reinforced by the model's noise robustness relative to LibriSpeech-trained baselines. The paper's authors interpret this as evidence that scaling weakly supervised pre-training — an approach with substantial precedent in natural language processing — has been comparatively underappreciated in speech recognition research to date {C015}. This is an interpretive claim about the field's prior emphasis, offered with lower confidence than the paper's directly measured results, and it should be read as such.

The scaling results (Section 5.4) sharpen this picture: gains are not free or unlimited. Both the dataset-size and model-size sweeps show rapid early improvement followed by diminishing returns, and the multitask/multilingual transfer crossover shows that combining tasks and languages in one model is not uniformly beneficial — it depends on how much data and compute the model has to work with. A reader deciding whether to build a large, broad, weakly supervised system versus a smaller, task-specific one should weigh both which scale of compute is available and which specific benchmark or use case matters most, since Section 5.3 shows that specialized systems can still exceed Whisper on some benchmarks.

### 6.2 Limitations

Several limitations bear directly on how far these results generalize. First, the training data is heavily English-skewed (approximately 65% English by hours), and most of the 96 other languages individually have under 1,000 hours {C014}. The strong data-scaling correlation for multilingual recognition (r² = 0.83, Section 5.2) means this imbalance predicts correspondingly uneven accuracy across languages; the evidence does not include a systematic per-language breakdown beyond the aggregate correlation and a few named outliers such as Welsh [MISSING RESULT: per-language WER breakdown beyond the aggregate Fleurs correlation and the Welsh case].

Second, label noise from the automated data-collection pipeline is a real and at least partially quantified concern: the Welsh-labeled-as-English case (Section 3.1) shows that automated language identification can misroute a language's entire allocated training budget, and the weaker translation-data correlation (r² = 0.24 vs. 0.83, Section 5.2) is attributed to this same class of labeling noise rather than to translation being intrinsically less scalable {C018}{L007}.

Third, the text-normalization methodology used throughout evaluation (Section 4.1) was developed jointly with the model it evaluates, creating a documented risk that it is tuned to Whisper's specific output patterns. The cross-check against an independent normalizer only partially mitigates this, since some dataset-specific differences remained {C013}{L001}{L005}.

Fourth, the evidence states that fine-tuning was not studied: Whisper is evaluated exclusively zero-shot, so how much further it could improve if adapted to a target domain — and hence its true performance ceiling — remains unknown [MISSING RESULT: fine-tuned Whisper performance on any benchmark in Section 5]. Relatedly, whether Whisper's robustness derives mainly from its trained encoder, its trained decoder acting as an audio-conditioned language model, or both, is raised but not tested {E022}.

Finally, in long-form transcription, the remaining errors described in the evidence are increasingly structural — complete hallucination of text unrelated to the audio, and repetition looping — rather than perceptual misrecognitions of individual words, and scaling model size has not clearly resolved these structural failure modes, unlike its clear benefit for word-level accuracy elsewhere {C007}{E031}. No quantitative frequency for these failures is reported [MISSING RESULT: quantified rate of hallucination/repetition-looping failures in long-form transcription].

## 7. Conclusion

This paper tested whether scaling weakly supervised, multitask, multilingual training data for speech — collected automatically from the internet, scaled roughly an order of magnitude beyond prior supervised speech work — produces a model that generalizes zero-shot across datasets, languages, and conditions. The evidence supports this: a single 680,000-hour-trained model, evaluated with no fine-tuning on any target benchmark, substantially reduces error relative to a matched supervised baseline across a diverse set of datasets, sets a new zero-shot translation state of the art, withstands acoustic noise better than dataset-specific baselines, and approaches professional human transcription quality on long-form audio, with all of these gains following a predictable relationship to training-data and model scale. At the same time, the same evidence shows this strategy is not unconditionally superior: coverage is uneven across languages, the translation-side data-scaling relationship is markedly weaker than the recognition-side one, and specialized supervised systems still win on specific benchmarks. Read together, the results support treating scale and diversity of weak supervision as one effective, comparatively underused lever for building robust speech systems, alongside — not as a wholesale replacement for — targeted data collection and task-specific methods for the settings this approach still under-serves.
