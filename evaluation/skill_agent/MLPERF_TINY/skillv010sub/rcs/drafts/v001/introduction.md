## 1. Introduction

Machine-learning inference is increasingly moving onto microcontroller-class (MCU) hardware, in a setting the community calls TinyML. TinyML devices run inference under roughly a milliwatt of power, are typically clocked between 10 and 250 MHz, and carry memory on the order of two orders of magnitude smaller than a smartphone-class ML device. Performing inference directly on such a device, near the sensor that produced the data, avoids the energy cost of sending that data elsewhere for processing, a cost that at this scale is far higher than the cost of computing on-device (Bouguera et al., 2018). This efficiency makes a class of applications possible that mobile-scale power budgets cannot reach: battery-powered devices that listen, watch, or monitor continuously rather than only when charged or carried.

Reaching this efficiency, however, requires co-optimizing every layer of the deployment stack at once: the dataset, the model, its numeric precision, the runtime, and the hardware it runs on. When every layer can move, isolating what any single change contributes -- a better model, a better compiler, a better chip -- is difficult without a common, fair way to measure the result, which in turn makes it hard to compare two proposed solutions or to tell whether the field is actually progressing.

A benchmark built for general microcontroller performance does not supply that common measure. CoreMark, the standard benchmark for MCU-class devices, is easy to port and uses real algorithms, but it does not profile a full program and does not represent machine-learning inference workloads {C009} (Gal-On and Levy, 2012). Benchmarks built for machine-learning inference do not supply it either: MLMark runs real inference workloads on edge-class devices, but its supported models need gigabytes of memory and produce runtimes far beyond MCU-class budgets, and it has no support for measuring power {C010} (Torelli and Bangale, n.d.). MLPerf's community-driven inference benchmark plans to add power measurement, but its current suite excludes MCUs and other resource-constrained platforms for lack of small benchmarks and compatible implementations {C011} (Reddi et al., 2019). None of these three efforts jointly measures accuracy, latency, and energy for ML inference on MCU-class hardware with a reproducible methodology {C012}. Section 3 examines each in more detail.

This paper asks whether that combination is achievable at all: can one benchmark suite fairly measure accuracy, latency, and energy for representative ML workloads on MCU-class hardware, while still letting a submitter's specific hardware or software contribution be demonstrated and directly compared against a shared reference?

We present MLPerf Tiny, a benchmark suite built around four representative workloads, each shipped as a complete, open reference implementation, together with a fixed protocol for measuring accuracy, latency, and energy {C001, C003}. Submissions are split into two divisions that trade comparability against flexibility: a closed division that fixes the dataset, model, and accuracy target so that hardware and software stacks can be compared head-to-head, and an open division that allows changes anywhere in the pipeline as long as every deviation from the reference is documented {C004}.

This paper makes three contributions. First, four open reference implementations, each pairing one dataset with one MCU-sized model and one quality target calibrated to the reference model's own measured accuracy {C001, C002}. Second, a measurement protocol and a two-division submission structure that jointly make accuracy, latency, and energy comparable on MCU-class hardware, closing a gap left by all three prior efforts examined above {C004, C012}. Third, evidence from a real first submission round showing that this design accommodates the hardware and software diversity of the field it targets {C006}.

The rest of the paper is organized around these questions. Section 2 gives the TinyML background an adjacent reader needs. Section 3 places MLPerf Tiny relative to the three prior benchmarks above. Section 4 describes the suite's design. Section 5 reports what happened when real submitters used it. Section 6 discusses what that first round does, and does not, establish, and where the design still has open tensions.
