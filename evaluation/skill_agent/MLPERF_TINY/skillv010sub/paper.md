# MLPerf Tiny: A Community Benchmark Suite for Accuracy, Latency, and Energy on Microcontroller-Class Machine Learning

## Abstract

Machine-learning inference is moving onto microcontroller-class hardware that runs on a fraction of a watt, a setting the community calls TinyML. Comparing hardware and software solutions for this setting fairly has been difficult: a general microcontroller benchmark does not run machine-learning workloads, and existing machine-learning inference benchmarks assume far more memory than a microcontroller provides or do not measure energy at all {C009, C010, C011, C012}. This paper presents MLPerf Tiny, an open benchmark suite that pairs four representative tasks -- keyword spotting, visual wake words, image classification, and anomaly detection -- with microcontroller-sized reference implementations and a fixed protocol for measuring accuracy, latency, and energy {C001, C003}. A closed submission division fixes the dataset, model, and accuracy target so that hardware and software stacks can be compared directly, while an open division allows changes anywhere in the pipeline provided they are documented {C004}. In the suite's first submission round, five submissions spanning both divisions and five distinct hardware and software categories -- from a general-purpose microcontroller to a field-programmable gate array trained with quantization-aware learning -- were measured against the reference implementations {C005}. This diversity is consistent with the modular design achieving its goal of accommodating TinyML's hardware and software heterogeneity while keeping results directly comparable {C006}. The evidence for this reading comes from one round with five submissions {L001}.

## 1. Introduction

Machine-learning inference is increasingly moving onto microcontroller-class (MCU) hardware, in a setting the community calls TinyML. TinyML devices run inference under roughly a milliwatt of power, are typically clocked between 10 and 250 MHz, and carry memory on the order of two orders of magnitude smaller than a smartphone-class ML device. Performing inference directly on such a device, near the sensor that produced the data, avoids the energy cost of sending that data elsewhere for processing, a cost that at this scale is far higher than the cost of computing on-device (Bouguera et al., 2018). This efficiency makes a class of applications possible that mobile-scale power budgets cannot reach: battery-powered devices that listen, watch, or monitor continuously rather than only when charged or carried.

Reaching this efficiency, however, requires co-optimizing every layer of the deployment stack at once: the dataset, the model, its numeric precision, the runtime, and the hardware it runs on. When every layer can move, isolating what any single change contributes -- a better model, a better compiler, a better chip -- is difficult without a common, fair way to measure the result, which in turn makes it hard to compare two proposed solutions or to tell whether the field is actually progressing.

A benchmark built for general microcontroller performance does not supply that common measure. CoreMark, the standard benchmark for MCU-class devices, is easy to port and uses real algorithms, but it does not profile a full program and does not represent machine-learning inference workloads {C009} (Gal-On and Levy, 2012). Benchmarks built for machine-learning inference do not supply it either: MLMark runs real inference workloads on edge-class devices, but its supported models need gigabytes of memory and produce runtimes far beyond MCU-class budgets, and it has no support for measuring power {C010} (Torelli and Bangale, n.d.). MLPerf's community-driven inference benchmark plans to add power measurement, but its current suite excludes MCUs and other resource-constrained platforms for lack of small benchmarks and compatible implementations {C011} (Reddi et al., 2019). None of these three efforts jointly measures accuracy, latency, and energy for ML inference on MCU-class hardware with a reproducible methodology {C012}. Section 3 examines each in more detail.

This paper asks whether that combination is achievable at all: can one benchmark suite fairly measure accuracy, latency, and energy for representative ML workloads on MCU-class hardware, while still letting a submitter's specific hardware or software contribution be demonstrated and directly compared against a shared reference?

We present MLPerf Tiny, a benchmark suite built around four representative workloads, each shipped as a complete, open reference implementation, together with a fixed protocol for measuring accuracy, latency, and energy {C001, C003}. Submissions are split into two divisions that trade comparability against flexibility: a closed division that fixes the dataset, model, and accuracy target so that hardware and software stacks can be compared head-to-head, and an open division that allows changes anywhere in the pipeline as long as every deviation from the reference is documented {C004}.

This paper makes three contributions. First, four open reference implementations, each pairing one dataset with one MCU-sized model and one quality target calibrated to the reference model's own measured accuracy {C001, C002}. Second, a measurement protocol and a two-division submission structure that jointly make accuracy, latency, and energy comparable on MCU-class hardware, closing a gap left by all three prior efforts examined above {C004, C012}. Third, evidence from a real first submission round showing that this design accommodates the hardware and software diversity of the field it targets {C006}.

The rest of the paper is organized around these questions. Section 2 gives the TinyML background an adjacent reader needs. Section 3 places MLPerf Tiny relative to the three prior benchmarks above. Section 4 describes the suite's design. Section 5 reports what happened when real submitters used it. Section 6 discusses what that first round does, and does not, establish, and where the design still has open tensions.

## 2. Background: The TinyML Setting

Before turning to related benchmarks, it is worth being concrete about the hardware class this paper targets. TinyML devices are microcontrollers, digital signal processors, and dedicated neural-network accelerators that typically run between about 10 MHz and 250 MHz and can perform inference using under 50 mW of power; their memory budget is commonly two orders of magnitude smaller than a smartphone-class machine-learning device. This is not simply mobile-scale ML made smaller: it changes which models fit at all, how much slack exists for a benchmark harness itself, and how power can be measured in the first place.

Four properties of this hardware class make benchmarking it different from benchmarking a server or a phone. Power is the defining constraint, but it is also hard to measure fairly: devices vary enormously in the power they draw, and what counts as part of the measurement -- the data path, pre-processing, chip peripherals, firmware -- is not always obvious. Memory is limited enough that models built for standard ML benchmarks, which can need gigabytes, do not fit, and a benchmark harness with any real overhead risks not fitting either. Hardware is heterogeneous: alongside general-purpose microcontrollers sit non-standard accelerator architectures that may lack a standard system clock or debug interface a benchmark might otherwise assume, so existing performance benchmarks generally need re-engineering before they can even run. Software is heterogeneous too, since achieving the highest efficiency on a given chip often means a custom tool chain; a benchmark that restricts the inference stack too tightly would unfairly penalize solutions built on a different one.

Two more pieces of vocabulary matter before the suite's design can be described. Quantization -- reducing a model's numeric precision, typically to 8-bit integers, to save memory and compute -- can be applied in two ways: post-training quantization (PTQ) compresses an already-trained model without retraining it, while quantization-aware training (QAT) retrains the model with the lower precision simulated during training, usually recovering more accuracy at the cost of needing the original training pipeline. As Section 4 describes, MLPerf Tiny's two submission divisions are defined partly by which of these two a submitter may use. Separately, one of the four benchmarks scores its output with the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC), a threshold-free accuracy metric that fits naturally whenever, as in anomaly detection, the model produces a continuous score rather than a class label.

## 3. Related Work

Comparing MCU-class hardware is not itself a new problem. The Embedded Microprocessor Benchmark Consortium (EEMBC)'s CoreMark benchmark has become the standard way to compare general MCU-class compute performance, in large part because it is simple to port and exercises real algorithms rather than synthetic instructions. It was never built to represent machine-learning inference, however: CoreMark does not profile a full program and does not accurately represent machine-learning inference workloads {C009} (Gal-On and Levy, 2012).

Two more recent efforts move closer to the target this paper cares about, but still miss it. MLMark benchmarks actual trained ML models on edge-class devices, which CoreMark does not attempt. Its supported models, however, need gigabytes of memory and produce runtimes far beyond what MCU-class hardware can spare, and, unlike CoreMark's companion power benchmark, MLMark has no support for measuring energy at all {C010} (Torelli and Bangale, n.d.). MLPerf's own community-governed inference benchmark is broader still and plans to add power measurement, but its current suite precludes MCUs and other resource-constrained platforms because it lacks small benchmarks and compatible implementations for them {C011} (Reddi et al., 2019).

None of the three jointly fits the combination this paper's research question asks about: ML-realistic workloads, MCU-class hardware, and energy as a first-class metric, delivered through a reproducible methodology {C012}. Closing that gap, rather than extending any one of the three individually, is what the rest of this paper describes; Section 4's reference models, all under 350 kilobytes, illustrate just how far below MLMark's and MLPerf Inference's assumed hardware scale the actual target sits.

## 4. The MLPerf Tiny Benchmark Suite

MLPerf Tiny's unit of comparison is the reference implementation: for each benchmark, an open-source package containing the training scripts used to produce the reference model, the pretrained model itself, and the C code that runs it. Every reference implementation runs its model in the TFLite format through TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers (TFLM), the runtime built for this hardware class (David et al., 2020), on a common reference board, and the entire suite, models included, is published on GitHub.

Table 1 lists the four benchmarks the v0.5 suite specifies {C001}. Keyword spotting recognizes one of a small set of spoken words, using the Speech Commands v2 dataset (Warden, 2018) and a small depthwise-separable convolutional network (Zhang et al., 2017). Visual wake words detects whether a person is present in an image, using a dataset built from MSCOCO 2014, a large general-purpose photo collection (Lin et al., 2014; Chowdhery et al., 2019), and a MobileNetV1 classifier (Howard et al., 2017). Image classification assigns one of ten object classes, using CIFAR-10 (Krizhevsky et al., 2009; Torralba et al., 2008) -- a dataset choice common enough in prior TinyML work (Fedorov et al., 2019) that it doubles as a historical reference point -- and a customized, three-stack ResNet (He et al., 2016). Anomaly detection flags abnormal machine sounds with an unsupervised, fully-connected autoencoder. It uses audio drawn from the DCASE2020 challenge's combination of two published machine-sound collections, ToyADMOS and MIMII (Koizumi et al., 2019; Purohit et al., 2019; Koizumi et al., 2020).

| Benchmark | Dataset | Reference model | Model size | Quality target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Keyword spotting | Speech Commands v2 | DS-CNN | 52.5 KB | 90% top-1 |
| Visual wake words | VWW (built from MSCOCO 2014) | MobileNetV1 | 325 KB | 80% top-1 |
| Image classification | CIFAR-10 | Customized ResNet | 96 KB | 85% top-1 |
| Anomaly detection | DCASE2020 (ToyADMOS + MIMII) | FC autoencoder | 270 KB | AUC 0.85 |

*Table 1. The four MLPerf Tiny v0.5 benchmarks, each pairing one dataset with one microcontroller-sized reference model and one numeric quality target. Model sizes are for the compiled TFLite reference model. Source: project/paper.txt Table 1 and Sections 4.1-4.4.*

Every quality target in Table 1 sits a small margin below what the reference model itself measures on its designated evaluation set {C002}. The keyword-spotting model reaches 91.6-91.7% accuracy but the target is 90%; the visual-wake-words model reaches about 86% against an 80% target; the image-classification model reaches 86.5% against an 85% target; and the anomaly-detection autoencoder reaches an AUC of 0.86-0.88 against a 0.85 target {C002}. The margin is deliberate: it is meant to absorb the small accuracy shifts that quantization and other cross-platform optimizations can introduce, so that a submission is not penalized for numerical differences that have nothing to do with the quality of its solution {C002}. Because each of these reference figures is a single measurement, with no retraining or seed variance reported, the exact width of margin that would be needed is not itself tested {L002}.

All three headline metrics are measured through the same runner software, connecting a host computer to the device under test. Latency is the median of five runs, each performing at least ten seconds and ten iterations of inference, reported as inferences per second {C003}. Energy uses an identical repeated-run protocol, additionally metering only the power delivered to the device's core, and reports the median energy per inference in microjoules {C003}. Accuracy runs a single pass over the full validation set and computes top-1 percent or AUC as appropriate, with a minimum threshold below which a submitted score is not considered valid {C003}. Because latency and energy always use the same repeated-run protocol, and because only core power counts toward the energy score, two submissions on different chips remain comparable even though their surrounding measurement hardware differs.

The suite resolves the comparability-versus-flexibility tension through two submission divisions {C004}. The closed division fixes the dataset, the model, and the quality target to those in Table 1, and permits only post-training quantization, with no retraining or weight replacement, so that a closed submission isolates the hardware and software stack from everything else {C004}. The open division allows a submitter to change the dataset, training procedure, or model, still scored against the same test set but without a mandatory accuracy bar, provided every deviation from the reference is documented {C004}. The two divisions therefore answer different questions: the closed division asks how fast, and how efficiently, a fixed piece of software runs on different hardware and tool chains; the open division asks how much better a workload can be solved once the model or data pipeline itself is allowed to change.

## 5. Evaluation: The v0.5 Submission Round

Whether this design actually accommodates the field's diversity, rather than merely being built to, can only be tested by real submitters using it. MLPerf Tiny's first submission round, v0.5, closed in June 2021, and every submission was checked for compliance and transparently reviewed by the group of submitters together with a review committee before being published {C005}.

Table 2 summarizes what the round produced. It drew five submissions, split four to one between the closed and open divisions, and spanning five distinct combinations of numeric format, software framework, and hardware {C005}.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Closed | none (fixed by rule) | INT-8 PTQ | TensorFlow Lite Micro | ARM microcontroller | Baseline performance on the reference platform |
| Closed | none (fixed by rule) | INT-8 PTQ | TensorFlow Lite Micro | RISC-V microcontroller | A RISC-V microcontroller customized for neural-network inference |
| Closed | none (fixed by rule) | FP-32 and INT-8 PTQ | LEIP Framework | Raspberry Pi 4 | A hardware-agnostic, software-only optimization toolchain |
| Closed | none (fixed by rule) | INT-8 PTQ | Syntiant TDK | Neural-network accelerator | Ultra-low-power hardware efficiency for deep neural networks |
| Open | training and model changed; dataset unchanged\* | INT-6/8 QAT | QKeras (training) / hls4ml (deployment) | FPGA | Rapid, end-to-end development of ML accelerators on reconfigurable hardware |

The five submissions to the v0.5 round (June 2021). \*Row 5's individual dataset/training/model marks did not survive text extraction from the source table intact; "dataset unchanged" is stated directly elsewhere in the source text, and "training and model changed" follows from this submission's use of quantization-aware training, which the closed-division rule (rows 1-4) prohibits. Source: project/paper.txt Table 2 and Section 6.2.*

Behind the one-line description in each row was a specific point in the deployment stack {C005}. One submitter used the round to show that its software development kit is hardware-agnostic and easy to use while preserving accuracy. A hardware vendor used it to show its neural-network accelerator's efficiency, which the authors attribute to a microarchitecture and datapath designed to avoid wait states. An academic research group used it to show the AI-compute capability of a microcontroller based on RISC-V, a free, open instruction-set architecture (Asanović and Patterson, 2014). A multi-disciplinary team used it to show hls4ml, an open-source workflow originally built for sub-microsecond inference at the Large Hadron Collider, repurposed here to compile a neural network onto an FPGA.

Three patterns held across all five submissions. INT-8 was the most common numeric format {C007}. Software frameworks ranged from an open-source interpreter to hardware-specific compilers, and measured power draw across the round's hardware spanned roughly microwatts to watts {C007}. A fourth pattern is notable for what did not happen: none of the five submissions modified the training dataset, even though a broader trend in machine learning has been moving toward data-centric development {C008}. Because this is a single round with five submissions, these patterns describe what this round's participants chose to do, not a demonstrated stable property of the field {L001}.

Read together, this range -- both divisions used, five different hardware categories, and a software-only entrant alongside four hardware-centric ones -- is consistent with the suite's modular, dual-division design achieving the goal it was built for {C006}. It let submitters at different layers of the deployment stack each show a distinct advantage, while the closed division's four entries remained directly comparable to one another and to the reference implementation {C006}. This answers the paper's research question affirmatively for the case actually tested. It does not establish that the pattern will hold in later rounds, that it would have failed under a non-modular alternative design, or that the closed division's current benchmarks, built mainly from fully-connected and convolutional layers, represent every architecture family a future TinyML benchmark might need {L001, L005}.

## 6. Discussion

The first submission round is consistent with a single suite letting markedly different submitters -- from a general-purpose microcontroller vendor to a team repurposing particle-physics tooling for an FPGA -- each show a distinct advantage under one shared, comparable protocol {C006}. That result is exactly the property the closed/open division split and the modular reference-implementation design were built to produce.

This is consistent with closing the gap described in Related Work by combining what no single prior effort combined: real ML workloads, MCU-class hardware, and energy measurement, delivered through a submission-and-review process similar to the one MLPerf's broader inference benchmark already uses (Reddi et al., 2019) {C012}. Where CoreMark measured the wrong workload, and MLMark and MLPerf Inference measured a closer workload on the wrong scale of hardware, MLPerf Tiny's reference models -- all under 350 kilobytes -- sit squarely inside the scale those two efforts do not reach {C012}.

There is early evidence that the suite is being put to use beyond this paper: MLPerf Tiny's benchmarks have already been used as a standard set of tasks in TinyML research, for instance in work on neural-architecture search for microcontrollers (Banbury et al., 2021) {C013}.

The same properties that make TinyML attractive carry a corresponding risk. Keeping data on the device that captures it, rather than transmitting it elsewhere, can widen access to machine learning and protect privacy; the same always-on, inexpensive sensing could equally be turned toward monitoring people without their consent, and the proliferation of cheap, single-purpose devices adds to electronic waste. A benchmark suite does not resolve these tensions, but a shared, community-developed one at least gives the field a common basis for discussing them.

### Limitations

Several limitations bound how far the round's diversity can be read. First, the evidence covers one round with five submissions and no parallel run of a non-modular alternative suite, so whether the diversity reflects the design specifically, rather than simply strong initial interest in a new community benchmark, cannot be separated with this evidence alone {L001}. Second, the reference accuracy and AUC figures behind each quality target's margin are each a single measurement; the paper does not report how much they would move under retraining, so the margin's robustness is untested {L002}. Relatedly, the specific latency and energy values the reference implementations achieved on their reference board are presented in the source material only as a figure, and could not be recovered as numbers for this account, so this paper can describe how latency and energy were measured but not what the reference implementations actually scored {L006}. Third, the closed division's four reference architectures are built mainly from fully-connected and convolutional layers; other families, such as recurrent networks, are reachable today only through the more flexible, less comparable open division {L005}.

A fourth limitation is a design tension the authors identify in their own suite, rather than one this analysis infers. Two of the four benchmarks, keyword spotting and anomaly detection, are naturally streaming tasks, but the measurement framework's host-to-device bandwidth makes a true streaming scenario difficult to reproduce without adding transfer delay. The suite instead excludes feature extraction from the measured window in the closed division, but doing so risks a degenerate case in which a submitter could define nearly the entire model as "feature extraction" to place it outside the measured portion. Including feature extraction instead would run into the opposite problem: in this non-streaming protocol, one second of audio implies roughly forty feature-extraction cycles for every inference cycle, over-counting the true cost of feature extraction {L004}. Neither choice is fully satisfactory, and the suite currently accepts the exclusion.

### Future directions

The authors describe three directions for closing these gaps. One is extending the suite to new application domains such as wearables, medical devices, and environmental monitoring, while keeping a stable long-term core subset so historical progress remains trackable {C014}. A second is widening the measurement scope to cover pre-processing and streaming behavior directly {C015}. A third is extending the closed division's architecture coverage beyond fully-connected and convolutional layers {C016}.

## 7. Conclusion

MLPerf Tiny gives the TinyML community something none of CoreMark, MLMark, or MLPerf Inference offered on its own: an open, modular benchmark suite that measures accuracy, latency, and energy together on microcontroller-class hardware, with a submission structure built to keep results comparable even as the hardware and software behind them vary widely {C001, C004, C012}. Its first real submission round is consistent with that design working as intended: five submissions, both divisions, and five different hardware and software categories, each built to show a different kind of advantage {C006}. What the round does not yet show is whether this pattern holds beyond a single round, on a wider range of architectures, or under a measurement protocol that fully resolves how to handle streaming and pre-processing {L001, L004, L005}. Those are the questions a suite that is already being reused in other TinyML research {C013} will need its next rounds to keep answering.
