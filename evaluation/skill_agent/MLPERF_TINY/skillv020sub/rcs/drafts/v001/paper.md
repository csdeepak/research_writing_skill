# Benchmarking Machine Learning Under a Milliwatt: The MLPerf Tiny Suite and Its First Round of Evidence

## Abstract

Continued progress on ultra-low-power, on-device machine learning has been limited by the lack of a widely accepted, reproducible benchmark for comparing the systems built for it {C033}. This paper presents MLPerf Tiny, a benchmark suite built by a large cross-industry and academic group specifically to close that gap {C007}. The suite fixes four reference tasks -- keyword spotting, visual wake words, image classification, and anomaly detection -- each with its own dataset, model, and accuracy or AUC threshold, and scores every submission on the same three axes: accuracy, latency, and energy {C007}. A closed division holds the model, dataset, and threshold fixed and allows only quantization, while an open division allows changing the model, training procedure, or dataset, so that the suite can balance strict comparability against room for innovation {C009}; a shared measurement protocol scores latency and energy as the median of five runs and includes an explicit rule against gaming the energy measurement with auxiliary power sources {C010}. All four reference implementations met their own accuracy targets on a shared reference board {C026}, and the suite's first external submission round, in June 2021, produced five results spanning an ARM microcontroller, a customized RISC-V microcontroller, a Raspberry Pi 4, a dedicated neural-network accelerator, and an FPGA, with measured power spanning roughly six orders of magnitude, from microwatts to watts {C027,C028}. This suggests a modular, three-metric design is flexible enough to make very different hardware and software stacks comparable, though the round also showed that no submitter changed the training dataset, so, in the authors' own words, TinyML practice in this round remained centered on models, frameworks, and hardware rather than data {C029,C030}. The authors themselves note that streaming and pre-processing measurement remain unresolved and that the closed division currently covers only fully-connected and convolutional architectures {L001,L002}.

## 1 Introduction

On-device machine-learning inference that runs at a power draw under one milliwatt -- a regime generally called TinyML -- is valuable for a specific reason beyond simply being small: performing inference on the sensor itself avoids the energy cost of sending data over a wireless link, which at this scale is far higher than the cost of the computation itself {C001}. That, together with the responsiveness and privacy of keeping data on-device, is what makes battery-powered, always-on smart applications possible in the first place. Realizing this potential, however, requires co-optimizing every layer of the deployment stack -- the model, the numerical precision, the runtime, the silicon -- and that kind of optimization is only useful if it can be measured and compared. Continued progress on TinyML systems has been limited specifically by the lack of a widely accepted, reproducible benchmark for making that comparison {C033}.

Part of why no such benchmark already existed is that the hardware this comparison has to cover is unusually constrained. TinyML devices -- microcontrollers, digital signal processors, and small neural-network accelerators -- typically run at somewhere between 10 and 250 MHz and perform inference using less than 50 mW of power, several orders of magnitude below the clock speed and power budget that phone- or GPU-class ML deployment can assume. A benchmark built for that larger scale simply will not run, or will not run meaningfully, on hardware this constrained.

Three existing benchmarks come close but each miss one property this setting requires. CoreMark (Gal-On and Levy, 2012), the standard benchmark for microcontroller-class devices, does not profile full programs and does not represent machine-learning inference workloads at all {C003}. MLMark does use real machine-learning inference workloads, but its supported models need far more memory and far longer runtimes than a microcontroller provides, and it has no power measurement of its own {C004}. MLPerf's own inference benchmark suite (Reddi et al., 2019), a broader community effort, explicitly excludes microcontrollers and other resource-constrained platforms, for lack of small enough benchmarks and compatible implementations {C005}. None of the three, in other words, is simultaneously representative of real ML inference, small enough to run on this hardware, and built to treat power as a first-class result rather than an afterthought -- and the authors state this gap directly, rather than leaving it implicit {C006}.

This paper asks an explicit question that follows from that gap: can one benchmark suite fit the extreme memory and power constraints of microcontroller-class devices, measure accuracy, latency, and energy together, and still let a fair comparison be made across a field whose hardware and software stacks are already highly heterogeneous?

The authors answer this with MLPerf Tiny, a suite built as the collaborative effort of more than fifty organizations from industry and academia {C007}. This paper's contributions are three points about that suite, each tied to a specific piece of evidence and taken up in turn. First, Sections 2 and 3 describe how the suite is designed -- its four reference tasks, its closed/open division structure, and its shared measurement protocol -- so that the comparison it produces is actually fair {C007,C009,C010}. Second, Section 4 reports whether the design holds up in practice: whether the reference implementations meet their own targets, and whether outside organizations could use the suite at all {C026,C027}. Third, Sections 5 and 6 discuss what that first round does and does not show about TinyML practice, and state the boundaries the authors themselves place on their own suite {C030,L001,L002,L003}.

## 2 Background and Related Work

Two things are worth fixing before describing the suite itself: why on-device inference at this scale is attractive, and why benchmarking it is unusually hard.

The appeal is fairly direct. Performing inference on-device and near the sensor that produced the data avoids the energy cost of transmitting that data wirelessly, a cost the authors describe as far higher, at this scale, than the cost of computing on it locally (Bouguera et al., 2018) {C001}. The same property brings faster responses (no round trip to a server) and better privacy (the raw data need never leave the device) {C001}. None of this is unique to any one application, which is exactly why a benchmark suite -- rather than a single point solution -- is the useful unit of comparison.

Designing that benchmark, though, runs into four constraints that interact with each other. Power measurement itself is hard to scope: TinyML devices vary enormously in how much power they draw, and it is not obvious what should count as part of the measurement once pre-processing, chip peripherals, and firmware are all in play. Memory is roughly two orders of magnitude tighter than the gigabyte-scale budgets conventional ML benchmarks assume, which means the benchmark's own overhead can be large enough to not fit, and that multiple levels of quantization need to be represented rather than assumed away. Hardware is heterogeneous by nature -- general-purpose microcontrollers sit alongside unconventional architectures such as event-based neural processors, and a device under test may lack features (a system clock, a debug interface) that most benchmarking infrastructure takes for granted. Software is just as heterogeneous, because TinyML systems are typically tied tightly to their own toolchains, especially once accelerators or DSPs are involved, so a benchmark that is too prescriptive about the software stack would unfairly penalize a different, equally valid deployment stack {C002}.

Measured against exactly these four constraints, the closest prior benchmarks each fail in a different, specific way. CoreMark's short, generic-code kernels are easy to port and widely adopted for microcontroller performance comparison, but they were never meant to represent machine-learning inference and do not {C003}. MLMark corrects that by using real inference workloads, but the models it supports assume gigabyte-scale memory and can take a long time to run -- neither of which a microcontroller can absorb -- and it has no power-measurement methodology of its own, despite EEMBC's companion CoreMark-based power benchmark existing for exactly this purpose {C004}. MLPerf's inference suite is the broadest and most community-governed of the three, but by the authors' own account it precludes microcontrollers outright, for lack of small enough benchmarks and compatible implementations {C005}. Put together, no benchmark available to the authors was simultaneously ML-representative, small enough for this hardware, and power-aware, and the authors state directly that a benchmark filling exactly that combination -- power as a first-class citizen, a methodology suited to TinyML -- was clearly needed {C006}.

## 3 The MLPerf Tiny Benchmark Suite: Design and Reference Implementations

MLPerf Tiny answers that need with four fixed reference benchmarks -- keyword spotting, visual wake words, image classification, and anomaly detection -- each pairing one dataset, one reference model, and one accuracy or AUC threshold with a measurement harness shared across all four, scoring latency, accuracy, and energy in a common way {C007}. Every reference implementation runs as a TFLite model on TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers (TFLM) (David et al., 2020), on a single shared reference board (a NUCLEO-L4R5ZI microcontroller board), so that the four benchmarks share not just a scoring rule but an actual runtime and device {C007}. Crucially, the suite keeps a complete, modifiable reference implementation for every benchmark, from training script to the on-device C code, specifically so that a submitter who improves only one layer of the stack -- a compiler, a quantization scheme, an accelerator -- can still demonstrate that improvement against a shared, common baseline rather than having to build an entire competing system {C008}. The suite also excludes pre- and post-processing from what is actually timed: it targets model inference specifically, so that the timing window means the same thing across very different applications.

That fairness guarantee is enforced through two submission divisions. In the closed division, a submission must use the same model, dataset, and quality target as the reference implementation, and it may apply only post-training quantization with the calibration data the suite provides; retraining or replacing weights is not allowed. The open division relaxes this: a submitter may change the model, the training procedure, or the dataset itself, and accuracy is still measured on the suite's own test set, but meeting the closed-division accuracy threshold is not required, and every deviation from the reference must be documented. This two-division split exists, in the authors' words, specifically to balance comparability against flexibility -- the closed division for a controlled, apples-to-apples comparison, the open division for demonstrating what a genuinely different approach can do {C009}.

The measurement protocol itself is shared across all four benchmarks. Latency is measured by running inference for at least ten seconds and at least ten iterations, five separate times, and reporting the median inferences-per-second (IPS) of those five runs as the score. Accuracy is measured once, as a single pass over the entire validation set, from which Top-1 percent or AUC is computed, and each benchmark has its own minimum accuracy that a submission must clear for the score to count. Energy uses the same five-run protocol as latency, but also measures the total energy drawn in the timing window and reports the median of five values, in microjoules per inference {C010}. One rule is worth calling out because it protects the energy number specifically: only a single power supply is allowed to power the device's core during the measurement, and no auxiliary batteries, supercapacitors, or energy harvesters are permitted, precisely so that these could not be used to defeat the measurement {C010}. The power drawn by the framework's own level-shifting hardware is excluded from the score, since it is a cost of the measurement apparatus, not of the device being tested {C010}.

Table 1 lays out the four benchmarks side by side before their individual design choices are walked through.

| Benchmark | Dataset (input size) | Reference model (size) | Quality target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keyword spotting | Speech Commands (49x10) | DS-CNN (52.5 KB) | 90% Top-1 |
| Visual wake words | VWW dataset (96x96) | MobileNetV1 (325 KB) | 80% Top-1 |
| Image classification | CIFAR-10 (32x32) | ResNet-derived (96 KB) | 85% Top-1 |
| Anomaly detection | ToyADMOS (5*128) | FC autoencoder (270 KB) | 0.85 AUC |

*Table 1. The four MLPerf Tiny v0.5 reference benchmarks. Every reference model is under 330 KB; quality targets are Top-1 accuracy for the three classification tasks and AUC-ROC for the unsupervised anomaly-detection task {C007}.*

Visual wake words asks whether at least one person appears in an image, a task the authors chose because it maps directly onto smart-doorbell and occupancy applications, and because its original reference network already fits most 32-bit microcontrollers {C011}. The dataset is drawn from MSCOCO 2014 (Lin et al., 2014), filtered so that a person occupies at least 2.5% of the frame and resized to 96x96, following the Visual Wake Words task definition (Chowdhery et al., 2019) {C012}. The reference model, a MobileNetV1 (Howard et al., 2017) with a 0.25 width multiplier and two output classes, reaches about 86% accuracy on this test set by the authors' own report {C012}. The closed-division threshold is set below that, at 80%, so that quantization and rounding differences between platforms do not fail an otherwise-good submission {C013}.

Image classification keeps CIFAR-10 (Krizhevsky et al., 2009) -- 60,000 32x32 color images across ten classes, split into five training batches and one 10,000-image test batch -- partly because prior TinyML work had already used CIFAR-10, letting new results relate to a historical point of reference (Fedorov et al., 2019) {C014}. The reference model is a reduced ResNetv1 (He et al., 2016): three residual stacks rather than the official four, and fewer, lower-stride filters overall; its first convolutional layer specifically omits the pooling step that would normally follow it, because of the input's low resolution {C015}. Evaluated with the suite's own runner on a 200-image test subset, this model reaches 86.5% accuracy, against which the authors set an 85% Top-1 threshold to accommodate minor quantization and optimization differences {C016,C017}.

Keyword spotting recognizes a small vocabulary of spoken words from Speech Commands v2 (Warden, 2018), a corpus of 105,829 utterances from 2,618 speakers split so that no speaker appears in more than one of the train/validation/test partitions. Ten of the corpus's thirty words are used directly; the remaining twenty are pooled with background noise into an "unknown" class, and a "silence" class is added, for twelve output classes in total. This design is chosen specifically to exercise both a closed-vocabulary command-phrase system's need to recognize multiple specific words and a wakeword system's need to reject an open set of background sound {C018}. The reference model is the small depthwise-separable convolutional network described in (Zhang et al., 2017), chosen because, at 38.6K parameters, it fits comfortably in microcontroller memory and uses layers most neural-network hardware already supports; the authors report it reaches 92.2% accuracy in their experiments, and separately that a quantized version of the same reference model reaches 91.6% on the full test set and 91.7% on the 1,000-utterance subset used for on-device evaluation {C019}. The accuracy requirement is set to 90%, below all three reported figures, specifically to allow for some variation in quantization strategy across submissions {C020}. Feature extraction itself is excluded from the timed measurement, with three pre-computed feature options offered to open-division submitters, because the authors judge it to be a small fraction of the benchmark's overall compute cost {C021}.

Anomaly detection is the suite's only unsupervised, non-classification task: separating normal machine sounds from anomalous ones, which matters industrially because failures are often rare and training data is often available only for normal operation. The dataset is drawn from DCASE2020, the 2020 Detection and Classification of Acoustic Scenes and Events challenge (Koizumi et al., 2020), itself built from the ToyADMOS (Koizumi et al., 2019) and MIMII recordings -- MIMII being an acronym for "malfunctioning industrial machine investigation and inspection" (Purohit et al., 2019). Although the source competition covers six machine types, MLPerf Tiny restricts itself to the toy-car type alone, on the grounds that the additional complexity of the other five would not benefit the benchmark {C022}. Training data is normal sound from seven different toy cars, 1,000 samples each, mixing machine sound with environmental noise. The reference model is a fully-connected autoencoder -- four 128-unit encoder layers and four 128-unit decoder layers around an 8-unit bottleneck -- chosen both because it is already a well-known baseline in the anomalous-sound-detection literature and because it gives the suite a model family built entirely from fully-connected layers, distinct from the convolutional models elsewhere in the suite {C022}. Audio is converted to a 128-band log-mel spectrogram with 32 ms frames, and the model runs repeatedly over a 5-frame sliding window, 640 values in and out {C022}. The reconstruction error is averaged over the central 6.4 seconds of each clip to give a single anomaly score {C022}. The authors score this with the threshold-free AUC-ROC metric rather than one that requires choosing an operating point, because they judge it to fit this problem's evaluation better {C023}. On a 248-sample evaluation set drawn from four machines, the fp32 reference model reaches an AUC of 0.88, falling to 0.86 once quantized; the suite's 0.85 threshold is set directly from those two values {C024,C025}.

## 4 Results

Two different kinds of evidence bear on whether this design actually works: whether the reference implementations meet their own targets when run, and whether organizations outside the authors could use the suite at all.

On the second question, the suite's first external submission round, held in June 2021, produced five results, publicly listed and each reproducible from published instructions {C027}. Table 2 summarizes what each submission targeted.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Closed | ARM microcontroller | INT-8 (post-training) | TensorFlow Lite Micro | Baseline performance on the reference platform |
| Closed | RISC-V microcontroller | INT-8 (post-training) | TensorFlow Lite Micro | A RISC-V microcontroller customized for neural-network inference |
| Closed | Raspberry Pi 4 | FP-32 and INT-8 (post-training) | LEIP Framework | A software-only optimization toolchain that is agnostic of the hardware |

| Open | FPGA | INT-6/8 (quantization-aware) | QKeras / HLS4ML | Rapid end-to-end development of ML accelerators on reconfigurable fabric |

*Table 2. The five submissions to the first (v0.5) MLPerf Tiny round. Per-submission accuracy/latency/energy scores are not given in the paper text and so are not reported here; see Section 6 {C027}.*

Four of the five results used the closed division and one used the open division. Together the five span a general-purpose microcontroller, a microcontroller built around a free, open instruction-set architecture called RISC-V (Asanovic and Patterson, 2014), a general-purpose single-board computer, a dedicated neural-network accelerator, and a reconfigurable FPGA fabric -- essentially one submission per major class of TinyML-relevant hardware available at the time. Each submitter's own framing lines up with the "what it demonstrated" column above. The accelerator's submitter, for instance, attributed its efficiency to a microarchitecture and datapath design that avoids wait-states, and the FPGA submission used "HLS4ML" -- high-level synthesis for machine learning -- a workflow originally built for sub-microsecond inference at the Large Hadron Collider, here framed as scaling down to low-power TinyML hardware from the opposite end of the performance spectrum.

Two patterns emerge from looking across the round rather than at any one submission. First, 8-bit integer numerics were the most common choice, which the authors attribute to it offering a performance gain with little cost to accuracy, and measured power draw across the five platforms spanned from microwatts to watts, a range of roughly six orders of magnitude {C028}. Second, and more pointedly, one thing did not vary across any of the five submissions: none of them modified the training dataset, even though the broader machine-learning field was, at the time, trending toward data-centric methods {C029}. The authors treat this as worth stating directly rather than as an incidental gap.

## 5 Discussion

Taken together, the first round answers the paper's opening question for the case it was actually able to test. A modular suite that scores accuracy, latency, and energy at once was sufficient to let five genuinely different hardware and software stacks -- a general microcontroller, a customized instruction-set microcontroller, a general-purpose computer, a dedicated accelerator, and reconfigurable hardware -- report results that a reader can meaningfully set side by side {C030}. That did not require forcing every submitter onto the same toolchain; it required only that every submitter share the same reference implementation, the same four tasks, and the same three scoring rules.

A plausible mechanism behind that result, though not one this round was designed to isolate on its own, is fairly direct: because the reference implementation is complete and swappable end to end, a submitter who wants to show off one layer of the stack -- a chip, a compiler, a quantization flow -- can replace only that layer and still produce a result that means the same thing as everyone else's {C030}. This reading is consistent with a hardware-agnostic software toolchain and a purpose-built accelerator both submitting meaningfully to the same benchmark despite having almost nothing else in common {C030}.

At the same time, the round's diversity was concentrated on particular layers of the stack and not on others. Every submission changed something about the hardware, the numerics, or the software toolchain; none changed the training data itself. The authors' own reading of this is that TinyML design, at least in this first round, remained focused on models, frameworks, and hardware, with a data-centric shift anticipated but not yet observed {C029,C030}. That reading is consistent with the round's composition, but it rests on a single round with five results, so it is better read as a first observation about where effort concentrated than as a settled property of the field.

For a reader outside TinyML, the more general point is this: a benchmark that measures three axes at once and lets any one layer of a deployment stack be swapped out appears to be a workable way to standardize comparison across a genuinely fragmented hardware and software landscape, without having to first agree on a single reference stack. Whether that same design choice would work as well in a more mature, more contested benchmarking setting -- where the incentive to game one axis at the expense of another is stronger -- is not something this first, cooperative round can show on its own. Beyond that first round, the suite's own authors report that it had already been adopted as a standard set of tasks in subsequent TinyML research (Banbury et al., 2021) and packaged into a public project on a TinyML development platform, which is a second, independent kind of evidence that the design was usable outside the group that built it {C031}.

## 6 Limitations

The authors themselves name two limitations that bound the suite's current design. First, they state that streaming and pre-processing measurement remains an open problem. Continuously streaming, time-domain tasks such as keyword spotting and anomaly detection are hard to reproduce faithfully given the limited bandwidth between the test runner and the device under test. Compounding this, the current choice -- excluding feature extraction from the timed window while letting submitters choose their own feature pipeline -- creates the possibility of a degenerate submission in which most of a model is simply relabeled "feature extraction" to avoid being timed. Including feature extraction in the timed window, conversely, would over-weight its cost relative to a real streaming deployment {L001}. Second, they state that the closed division's models are, for now, mainly fully-connected and convolutional architectures in well-established shapes, and that broader architectural coverage -- recurrent networks are their example -- and long-term, cross-version stability of the benchmark are both stated goals for future versions rather than properties this suite has already demonstrated {L002}.

The authors also name a broader caveat that is not specific to any one measurement choice: the same properties that let TinyML democratize access to machine intelligence and keep users' data on-device could, they note, be misused to track or monitor people without their consent, and the low cost of the devices themselves can contribute to electronic waste {L003}.

Additional caveats. Beyond what the authors state directly, the evidence available for this paper comes from a single submission round of five results, evaluated on one reference board {L004}. The paper's own text does not report per-submission accuracy, latency, or energy scores (those are held in an external results repository outside the material available here), nor does it report run-to-run variance for the reference implementations' own latency or energy scores, or seed-to-seed variance for any of the reported reference-model accuracy figures {L004,L005}. This limits how strongly the round's diversity can be read as evidence that the design will keep working as more submitters, and eventually more competitive incentives, are added, and it limits how precisely any single number reported in Sections 3-4 should be treated.

## 7 Conclusion

MLPerf Tiny gives the TinyML field something it did not have: a benchmark suite that fits microcontroller-class memory and power constraints, scores accuracy, latency, and energy together rather than one at a time, and is built so that a submitter can show the value of one layer of the stack without re-deriving the whole system. Its reference implementations met their own accuracy targets on a shared board, and its first external round turned that design into five genuinely comparable results across five different kinds of hardware, which is itself evidence that the suite functions as intended for at least one round {C030}. What that round leaves open is, by the authors' own account, exactly where the interesting next questions sit: whether future rounds bring architectures beyond fully-connected and convolutional networks, whether the benchmark can be kept stable across versions while still growing to new application domains, and, distinctly, whether any future submission finally treats the training data itself as something worth optimizing {C032}.
