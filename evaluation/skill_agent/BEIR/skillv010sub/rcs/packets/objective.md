In the authors' words, this paper is meant to show that whether a retrieval system works well on the dataset it was trained on says little about whether it will work on a genuinely different retrieval task or domain it received no training data for — a "zero-shot" setting that most real applications of retrieval actually face. To make that testable at all, we built BEIR, a benchmark that pulls together 18 existing datasets spanning 9 very different retrieval tasks and domains (from biomedical search to fact-checking to argument retrieval) into one common data format and one scoring rule, and we used it to compare ten representative retrieval systems built on five different architectural approaches. A reader should come away understanding: that no single architectural approach wins everywhere; that the classical, untrained BM25 method remains a surprisingly strong baseline even though it clearly loses to neural methods when both are tested on the same training dataset; that the systems which generalize best are also the most computationally expensive; and that part of why some systems look weaker than others is not just the system itself but how the test data they are being judged on was built and annotated in the first place. The paper is offered as a tool and a cautionary result together: a way for future researchers to actually check generalization instead of assuming it, and a demonstration that today's dominant way of measuring retrieval progress — a single in-domain score — is not telling the whole story.
